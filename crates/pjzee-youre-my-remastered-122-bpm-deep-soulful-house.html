1. PJZEE – "You're My (Remastered)" [JuizeClub]

BPM & Groove Pocket Characteristics

Operating at an exact 122 BPM tempo, PJZEE's "You're My (Remastered)" features a meticulously engineered groove pocket that sits comfortably between European minimal-deep house and classic New York/New Jersey soulful house. The rhythmic backbone leverages an unyielding four-on-the-floor kick dialed with moderate attack and prolonged low-end decay, paired with syncopated Latin-influenced percussion (woodblocks, dry cabasas, and subtle shakers) that gives the track an undeniable forward rolling motion without cluttering the upper frequencies.

Bassline Architecture, Synths & Vocal Production

The low-end foundation of "You're My (Remastered)" relies on a warm, round analog bassline oscillating primarily in the 50 Hz to 120 Hz region. Its filter envelope is tuned to leave ample headroom for the rhythmic elements while maintaining weight on large-scale club subwoofers. Above the low end sits a lush chord progression voiced on a Fender Rhodes electric piano, colored with vintage chorusing and wide stereo imaging. Melodic guitar licks weave in and out alongside expansive, airy synthesizer pads.

The centerpiece of the track is an intimate, dry contralto vocal glide. Rather than relying on hyper-processed pitch shifts or drenched delays, the vocal production favors natural vocal timbre, presence, and close proximity micro-dynamics, allowing the emotional weight of the phrase to sit directly in front of the listener's ear.

Cue-In, Cue-Out & Set Context

For a Late-Night Deep House Club Session, introduce "You're My (Remastered)" around the 02:00 AM mark. Cue the track at intro bar 16 where the syncopated percussion layers over the kick drum. Blend smoothly across 32 bars beneath an outgoing minimalist dub track, slowly opening the channel high-pass filter (HPF) as you swap the basslines at bar 33. For a Sunset Terrace Soulful Blend, utilize the track's instrumental break as your focal mix point, allowing the Rhodes keys and guitar licks to breathe over ambient outdoor room acoustics before the kick re-engages.

2. Kerri Chandler – "You're in My System"

BPM & Groove Pocket Characteristics

Pitched precisely to 122 BPM, Kerri Chandler's historic anthem showcases the quintessential East Coast swing. The track’s micro-timing relies on heavy MPC swing applied to open hi-hats and hard-hitting 909 rimshots, pulling the listener forward while maintaining a laid-back, infectious swagger.

Bassline Architecture, Synths & Vocal Production

The signature Kerri bass sound utilizes analog subtractive synthesis—a customized square/sawtooth hybrid processed through punchy VCA envelopes that strike aggressively on the downbeat. The vocal delivery is classic, raw, and saturated with reel-to-reel tape warmth, sitting nestled inside short room reverbs and snappy slapback delays. Chords are punched through raw organ patches that deliver sustained dynamic pressure to the dancefloor.

Cue-In, Cue-Out & Set Context

This is your ultimate late-night weapon. Positioned directly inside the subterranean depths of a club set, cue the track during the outro of a darker 9A cut like "You're My (Remastered)". Utilizing an 8A subdominant harmonic shift, bring in Chandler’s 909 kick on an 8-bar phase while scooping low frequencies at 100 Hz. Once the vocal stab hits, execute a hard low-end swap on the 1 of the 16th bar to electrify the room.

3. Louie Vega – "Elements of Life"

BPM & Groove Pocket Characteristics

The groove pocket does not rely purely on digital quantization; instead, it swings with live percussionists playing timbales, congas, and shekeres around a sturdy four-on-the-floor kick, creating organic polyrhythmic tension that energizes open-air festival spaces.

Bassline Architecture, Synths & Vocal Production

Featuring live electric bass with clear upper-register articulation and natural fret dynamics, the track yields unmatched musicality. Cue-In, Cue-Out & Set Context

The transition from PJZEE’s "You're My (Remastered)" (9A) into Vega's "Elements of Life" (9B) represents a textbook relative major harmonic modulation—transforming moody, introspective late-afternoon tension into broad daylight joy and ecstatic celebration.

BPM & Groove Pocket Characteristics

Harmonic Mixing & Camelot Wheel Transitions (Centered Around 9A / 122 BPM)

Harmonic mixing is critical when blending vocal-heavy and melody-driven deep and soulful house tracks. Centering the crate at 9A (E minor) at 122 BPM unlocks four distinct harmonic trajectories across the Camelot wheel:

Recipe 1: The Relative Major Emotional Lift (9A → 9B)

  • Track A: PJZEE – "You're My (Remastered)" (9A / E minor)
  • Track B: Louie Vega – "Elements of Life" (9B / G Major)
  • Acoustic & Psychological Effect: Transitions the room from deep, introspective introspection into soaring, spiritual euphoria without altering root pitch tonal compatibility.
  • Execution Protocol:
    1. Wait until Track A reaches the final vocal phrase where the arrangement strips back to the dry vocal glide, Rhodes chords, and Latin percussion (Bar 64 of outro).
    2. Engage a 1/2 beat ping-pong delay on Track A's vocal tail.
    3. Start Track B on the downbeat of its intro percussion buildup. Low EQ cut completely on Track B; Mid EQ set at -2 dB.
    4. Over 32 bars, gradually open Track B’s Mid EQ to weave in the live congas. At Bar 33, perform an immediate hard-cut low-end swap on the downbeat as the live bass of Track B drops.

Recipe 2: The Dominant Energy Boost (9A → 10A)

  • Track A: PJZEE – "You're My (Remastered)" (9A / E minor)
  • Track B: Folamour – "Devoted to U" (10A / B minor)
  • Acoustic & Psychological Effect: Moving clockwise around the Camelot wheel (+1 fifth) injects subtle energetic tension, raising dancefloor anticipation as twilight shifts into night.
  • Execution Protocol:
    1. Loop an 8-bar percussion-heavy section of Track A after the primary vocal finishes.
    2. Cue Track B at the start of its filtered string intro.
    3. Apply a high-pass filter (HPF) on Track B up to 350 Hz, aligning the kick drums.
    4. Slowly sweep Track B's HPF downward across 16 bars while simultaneously lowering Track A's Channel High EQ to remove competing shakers.
    5. On the 1 of the 17th bar, swap channel basslines cleanly using a fast 1-beat crossfader flick.

Recipe 3: The Subdominant Subterranean Slide (9A → 8A)

  • Track A: PJZEE – "You're My (Remastered)" (9A / E minor)
  • Track B: Kerri Chandler – "You're in My System" (8A / A minor)
  • Acoustic & Psychological Effect: Moving counter-clockwise (-1 fourth) deepens the mood, pulling dancers further down into a hypnotic, subterranean club trance.
  • Execution Protocol:
    1. Wait for Track A's breakdown where the kick drum cuts out, leaving the intimate contralto vocal and Rhodes keys.
    2. Cue Track B's 909 kick and rimshot groove with low EQ at 9 o'clock.
    3. Blend Track B under the Rhodes chords of Track A. Because E minor and A minor share core scale degrees (G, A, C, D, E), the melodic dissonance is minimal.
    4. As Chandler’s iconic square bassline is introduced, sharply isolate Track A's low and mid frequencies, leaving only the airy pads of "You're My" to wash out over Chandler’s driving groove.

Frequently Asked Questions (DJ Crate & Mixing Insights)

Why is 122 BPM considered the definitive tempo sweet spot for bridging Sunset Soulful House and Late-Night Deep House?
122 BPM sits squarely at the intersection of human resting-to-dancing heart rate transition zones and analog club acoustic physics. It is leisurely enough to allow complex jazz chords (Rhodes 7ths and 9ths), uncompressed live percussion, and expressive vocal performances to breathe without feeling rushed, yet brisk enough to maintain the rolling kinetic forward drive required for peak-hour late-night warehouse dancefloors.
How does the production and mixdown of PJZEE's "You're My (Remastered)" facilitate long club blends?
The remastered mixdown features strict low-end frequency management: the sub-bass energy is tightly focused between 50 Hz and 90 Hz, leaving a clear dynamic pocket in the lower midrange (150–400 Hz). This architectural spacing prevents the mix from muddying when layered over the incoming kicks or percussion stems of benchmark tracks like Kerri Chandler or Folamour, permitting extended 32-bar to 64-bar fader blends.
What harmonic mixing techniques should DJs avoid when transitioning out of a 9A Camelot key track?
DJs should avoid multi-step diagonal jumps across the Camelot wheel (such as jumping directly from 9A to 3B or 11B) during vocal-heavy phrases. Colliding minor seconds and tritone clashes between prominent vocal lines and unmasked piano chords create severe harmonic dissonance on high-fidelity club sound systems. When a non-harmonic transition is required, strip the outgoing track down to its dry drum stem before introducing the incoming musical motif.
